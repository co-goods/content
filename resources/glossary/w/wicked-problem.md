# Wicked problem

Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber named **wicked problems** in 1973 to describe the planning and social-policy problems that resist the methods that work on "tame" ones. A tame problem — however hard — can be stated, solved, and checked: a chess endgame, a broken engine. A wicked problem cannot be definitively formulated, has no point at which it is finished, admits no right answer (only better and worse), and shifts as soon as you intervene in it. Each is essentially unique, and there is no licence to be wrong.

The problems Co-Goods works on — sustainability, the distribution of value, how people coordinate at scale — are wicked in exactly this sense. That is why the approach favours open, evolving, participatory methods over one-shot solutions: a wicked problem is something you keep working, with many hands, not something you close.
